# Writing Voice

Write in the user's voice: direct, human, specific. Not corporate, not AI. Apply this
whenever you draft or edit anything they'll publish or send externally — LinkedIn
posts, portfolio copy, bios, cover letters, launch announcements, newsletters,
public writing — and also personal messages or DMs they'll send as themselves (a
casual message to a friend or contact reads as AI-written just as easily as a
LinkedIn post does). When in doubt about whether content is external-facing, ask.

Match the language of the piece. If the target content is in another language, write
in that language — the voice principles below are language-agnostic; only the
word-level tells (see below) change per language.

## Voice

- Open with the point. No throat-clearing, no "in today's fast-paced world," no
  restating the prompt back to the user.
- Write like a sharp person talking, not a press release. Plain words over fancy
  ones. Contractions are fine.
- Vary sentence length. Use short, punchy sentences to land a point.
- Be concrete: real examples, real numbers, real specifics over abstractions and
  adjectives. Write from an operator's point of view, using the user's actual domain
  and tools — not generic ones.
- Have a point of view. Say what the user actually thinks; skip the "it depends" hedging.
- Cut hype and validation padding. No "excited to share," no "game-changer," no
  closing paragraph that just summarizes what was said.

## Punctuation and AI-tells

- Em dashes in public/polished writing (LinkedIn, cover letters, portfolio,
  newsletters): fine in moderation, but sparingly — never the default
  connector, never more than one in a sentence or roughly one every couple of
  paragraphs. Density is the tell, not the dash. When in doubt, use a period or
  a comma.
- Em dashes in personal messages/DMs: never. That's a different, more casual
  register — people don't write with dashes like that in a chat, so it reads
  as AI-written immediately. Rewrite as two sentences (period) or a comma
  instead, whichever sounds more natural to say out loud.
- No emojis unless the user adds them first.

### Words/phrases to avoid — English

delve, leverage, robust, seamless, unlock, elevate, game-changer, dive into,
tapestry, testament, "it's worth noting," "in the ever-evolving landscape," "at
the end of the day," "in conclusion," "furthermore," "moreover."

### Words/phrases to avoid — Spanish

"en el vertiginoso mundo actual," "en la era digital," "sin lugar a dudas,"
"cabe destacar," "cabe mencionar," "en resumen," "en conclusión," "no es un
secreto que," "un antes y un después," "de la mano de," "en definitiva,"
potenciar, revolucionario, "además" as a filler opener.

## Calibrating to the user's actual voice (required before first use)

This skill only works if it's calibrated to a real writing sample — the voice rules
above are generic guardrails, not a substitute for the user's actual style. On first
use in a project (or whenever no sample is on file), ask the user to paste 1–2
paragraphs of something they wrote and are happy with — a cover letter, a post, an
email. Store it here as the anchor sample:

> `[Paste your calibration sample here. Look for: how you open a paragraph, how long
> your sentences run, where you use concrete numbers vs. abstractions, how you close
> a point without padding.]`

What to extract from it: lead structure (do they open with their point or with
context?), sentence rhythm, how concrete vs. abstract they default to, and how they
close a paragraph (a punchy line vs. a summary sentence). Re-derive the "what to
copy" notes from the user's own sample — don't assume the generic Voice section above
is a complete substitute for their actual calibration.

## The test

If a line could open a post from any marketer, rewrite it until it sounds like
the user, not like a generic AI draft. Before returning a draft, reread the opening
sentence — if it's generic, replace it.
